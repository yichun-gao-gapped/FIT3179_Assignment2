How Much of Australia Is Really Protected?

Australia protects more than one-fifth of its terrestrial environment. But a single national percentage cannot tell us where that protection lies, what kinds of landscapes it covers, or whether Australia's ecosystems are represented equally.

Data snapshot: Unless otherwise stated, CAPAD-based figures refer to 30 June 2024.

At first glance, 22.57% sounds reassuring. In 2024, just over 22 hectares in every 100 were formally protected. But this national figure alone cannot tell us where that protection is, what it protects, or how evenly it represents Australia's ecosystems. 01

Where Is Australia Protected?

The first complication appears on the map. Australia's 22.57% is not spread evenly across the continent. Protection changes dramatically depending on whether we look at individual reserves, state borders or ecological regions.

Protected land forms a highly uneven national pattern. Thousands of protected areas appear across Australia, but the largest reserves are concentrated in particular landscapes. Many smaller areas cluster along the east, south-east and south-west, while several enormous reserves dominate parts of the interior.

State borders reveal an even sharper contrast. The ACT and Tasmania protect relatively large shares of their land, while Queensland and New South Wales protect much smaller proportions. A national percentage therefore describes no single Australian experience.

Only five of the eight jurisdictions exceed the national protected share. The ACT, Tasmania, Western Australia, South Australia and the Northern Territory sit above Australia's 22.57% national figure, while Victoria, New South Wales and Queensland remain below it. The same national total is therefore built from very different regional outcomes.

Source: CAPAD 2024 terrestrial protected-area statistics.

But administrative borders still do not tell the whole story. IBRA bioregions are large natural regions that share similar climate, geology, landforms, vegetation and species. Viewed through these ecological rather than political boundaries, a different pattern emerges: some bioregions are extensively protected, while neighbouring ecosystems remain far less represented.

Source: DCCEEW Interim Biogeographic Regionalisation for Australia (IBRA 7.1) and CAPAD 2024.

02

What Does Protection Look Like?

Location is only part of the story. “Protected” does not mean the same thing everywhere. IUCN management categories describe different conservation purposes, ranging from strict nature protection to areas where conservation is combined with sustainable use. Governance tells us who is responsible for managing those landscapes.

Australia's protected estate is not built around a single conservation model. Category VI, which allows sustainable use alongside long-term conservation, accounts for the largest share. National parks, shown as Category II, form another major component, while stricter categories make up smaller shares of the national system.

Nor is protection delivered by government alone. Community governance, which includes Indigenous Protected Areas, accounts for a major share of protected land alongside government, joint and private arrangements. Australia's conservation network is therefore also a network of different custodians.

03

Where Are the Ecological Gaps?

This is where the national percentage begins to break down. DCCEEW reports protection levels across Australia's 89 IBRA bioregions using a 10% threshold: 62 have more than 10% protected, while 27 remain below 10%. We use the same reporting reference here to examine where ecological representation remains weakest.

The gaps are scattered across the continent rather than confined to one region. Bioregions below the 10% reporting reference appear across northern, eastern, southern and inland Australia. Expanding the national total alone would therefore not guarantee that these ecological gaps disappear.

Source: CAPAD 2024 and IBRA 7.1. The 10% threshold follows DCCEEW's reporting of bioregion protection levels.

Even underprotected bioregions are not underprotected in the same way. Their mix of conservation categories differs substantially. Two regions with similarly low overall protection can therefore contain very different forms of protected land.

Some ecological gaps are far deeper than others. Sturt Plateau sits below 1% protected, while several other mainland and Tasmanian bioregions remain well below the 10% reporting reference. 04

How Is the Protected-Area Network Structured?

Even the word “reserve” hides enormous differences in scale. Australia's protected-area network is built from thousands of small records and a much smaller number of landscapes covering hundreds of thousands or even millions of hectares.

Most recorded protected areas are small. Of the 11,075 distinct protected-area records in this spatial extract, approximately 53.6% cover less than 100 hectares. Yet at the opposite extreme, a handful extend across millions of hectares. Australia's protected-area network is therefore built from very different scales.

Who manages a reserve is also associated with the scale at which it operates. 05

Does Scale Determine Protection?

A simple explanation would be that larger ecosystems receive more protection. The data do not support that assumption. Large and small bioregions appear at both high and low protection levels, suggesting that physical scale alone cannot explain ecological representation.

Bigger does not mean better protected. High and low protection levels occur across a wide range of bioregion sizes. Some large ecosystems remain poorly represented, while others achieve much higher protected shares. Size alone is clearly not the deciding factor.

Yet a few individual parks operate at an extraordinary scale. Munga-Thirri—Simpson Desert covers about 3.61 million hectares in this spatial extract, more than twice the next-largest record. A small number of enormous parks can therefore make a substantial contribution to total protected hectares without resolving every ecological gap.

Source: DCCEEW CAPAD spatial service. Ranking includes records designated as National Park in this spatial extract.

06

Is Protected-Area Coverage Growing?

On one measure, the direction is clear: Australia is protecting more land. Formal terrestrial protected-area coverage has expanded substantially over the past two decades. But growth in area does not automatically solve the geographic and ecological inequalities revealed above.

Formal protected-area coverage has more than doubled since 2004. Coverage rose from 10.52% in 2004 to 22.57% in 2024, passing the former 17% Aichi benchmark. Australia's current 30 by 30 commitment aims for 30% of land to be protected or conserved by 2030. The challenge ahead is therefore not only to increase the total, but to make that growth ecologically meaningful.

Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Measuring What Matters – Protected Areas; CAPAD; DCCEEW 30 by 30.

THE BIG PICTURE

More Than a Percentage

Australia protects a substantial and growing share of its terrestrial environment. At first glance, 22.57% offers a simple answer to the question of how much land is protected.

But the deeper we look, the less simple that number becomes. Protection is distributed unevenly between jurisdictions and ecological regions, varies in management purpose and governance, and is shaped by thousands of small reserves alongside a handful of enormous landscapes. Some ecosystems remain poorly represented even while others are extensively protected.

The real measure of protection is therefore not only how much land Australia sets aside, but whether that protection reaches the ecosystems and landscapes that need it.
